About Our Converter Categories

Basic Converters

Measurement itself dates back to ancient civilisations - the Egyptians used cubits, the Romans paces, and the medieval world a patchwork of local units. The metric system, born from the French Revolution of 1795, finally brought global coherence. Basic conversions - length, weight, temperature, area, volume, time, speed and more - remain the most frequently needed in daily life, education, cooking, construction and travel.

Heat Converters

Thermodynamics took shape in the 19th century as scientists like Carnot, Joule and Kelvin sought to understand steam engines and the nature of energy. The competing calorie, BTU and joule traditions reflect those different industrial heritages. Today, heat and thermal quantities are central to engineering design, climate science, HVAC systems and everything from cooking to nuclear reactors.

Engineering Converters

Classical mechanics, formalized by Newton in the 17th century, gave engineers a universal language of force, pressure, torque and power. The industrial revolution then demanded practical units - PSI for steam boilers, horsepower for engines, RPM for machinery. Engineering converters bridge the metric SI system with the imperial and customary units still common in manufacturing, construction and aerospace.

Light Converters

Photometry - the science of measuring light as perceived by the human eye - emerged in the 18th century with Bouguer and Lambert. The candela became one of the seven SI base units in 1948, defined by the luminous intensity of a specific candle flame and later by a precise radiometric standard. Light measurement is essential in photography, architecture, display calibration, horticulture and vision research.

Fluids Converters

Fluid mechanics traces its roots to Archimedes and was systematised by Bernoulli, Euler and Navier in the 18th and 19th centuries. Measuring flow, viscosity and pressure accurately is critical in hydraulics, chemical processing, medicine and environmental engineering. Units range from the everyday litre per minute to the highly specialised Poise, Darcy and molar flow quantities used in research and industry.

Electricity Converters

The modern electrical unit system was established in the late 19th century, honouring pioneers such as Volt, Ampere, Ohm and Faraday. Electricity underpins virtually every aspect of modern civilisation, and precise unit conversion is essential for circuit design, power systems, electronics, telecommunications and energy management - from millivolts in sensors to megawatts in power grids.

Magnetism Converters

Electromagnetism was unified by Maxwell in 1865, but practical magnetic units evolved separately in the CGS and SI traditions, leaving a legacy of parallel systems - Gauss alongside Tesla, Oersted alongside Ampere per metre. Magnetism quantities are indispensable in motor design, magnetic storage, medical imaging (MRI), particle accelerators and materials science.

Radiology Converters

Radiation measurement began with Roentgen's discovery of X-rays in 1895 and grew rapidly through the nuclear age. Early units like the rad and rem gave way to the SI gray and sievert as international safety standards tightened. Accurate dosimetry is vital in diagnostic imaging, radiotherapy, nuclear power, radiation protection and environmental monitoring.

Other Converters

Not every quantity fits a classical physics category. Data storage units emerged with the digital revolution - bits and bytes were defined in the 1950s and have scaled from kilobytes to zettabytes within a single lifetime. This section also covers sound levels, numeric bases, screen resolution, lumber dimensions and other specialised measurements that have become essential in the modern world.
